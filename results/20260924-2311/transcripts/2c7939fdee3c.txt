With that caveat, here are methodological considerations often raised about MSK-CHORD-style real-world clinico-genomic cohorts, several of which apply to this study specifically:

**1. Cohort ascertainment / selection bias**
Patients in MSK-CHORD were sequenced via MSK-IMPACT, which is preferentially ordered for patients with advanced, relapsed, or clinically complex disease, and for those able to access a tertiary referral center in New York. This skews the cohort toward more aggressive or treatment-refractory disease and toward patients with insurance/access enabling referral to MSK — so prevalence and outcome estimates may not generalize to community oncology populations.

**2. Immortal time bias / left truncation**
Because sequencing often occurs after diagnosis (sometimes well after, on a metastatic or recurrent sample), patients who died or progressed rapidly before a biopsy/sequencing could occur are systematically excluded. This can bias survival analyses toward "healthier" survivors of early disease — a recognized challenge in retrospective clinico-genomic real-world datasets, not unique to MSK-CHORD but relevant to its outcome analyses.

**3. Heterogeneous sample timing (primary vs. metastatic, pre- vs. post-treatment)**
Genomic profiles were taken at variable points in each patient's disease course and treatment history. Mutational and copy-number profiles can shift under treatment pressure (e.g., acquired resistance alterations), so pooling samples from different treatment lines/timepoints as if they reflect a single time-invariant genomic state can confound genotype-outcome associations.

**4. Retrospective, non-randomized treatment assignment**
Associations between genomic alterations and treatment response/outcome are observational — treatment choice was not randomized, so confounding by indication (sicker patients or specific molecular subtypes preferentially receiving certain regimens) can masquerade as a genomic-outcome association.

**5. Panel and detection limitations**
MSK-IMPACT is a targeted panel, not whole-genome/whole-exome sequencing, so structural variants, some fusions, and non-panel genes are not comprehensively captured; this can undercount certain alteration classes and bias comparisons across genes with uneven panel coverage/version changes over time (the panel itself expanded across versions during the accrual period, which can introduce gene-specific ascertainment differences by calendar year).

**6. Single-institution generalizability**
As a single-center (MSK) cohort, practice patterns, testing thresholds, and patient demographics (e.g., insurance status, race/ethnicity distribution, referral patterns) may differ substantially from population-based or multi-institutional cohorts, limiting external validity of prevalence and prognostic claims.

**7. Multiple testing across many gene-outcome associations**
With thousands of genes and dozens of cancer types tested for association with outcomes, there is elevated risk of false-positive "significant" findings unless rigorously corrected — the degree of correction and how conservatively it was applied is a common point of scrutiny in papers of this scale.

**8. Missing/incomplete clinical annotation**
Real-world clinical data (treatment lines, response, comorbidities) are often abstracted retrospectively from EHRs, introducing potential misclassification or incompleteness relative to prospective clinical trial data collection.
