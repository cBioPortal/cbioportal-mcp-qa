# cBioPortal FAQ Guide

Curated answers to frequently asked general questions about cBioPortal. Source: [cBioPortal FAQ](https://docs.cbioportal.org/user-guide/faq/).

## What is cBioPortal?

cBioPortal for Cancer Genomics is an open-access, open-source resource for interactive exploration of multidimensional cancer genomics data sets. It was originally developed at Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center (MSK) and is now maintained by a multi-institutional team.

## History

- **2008**: cBioPortal first became available online.
- **2012**: First major publication — Cerami et al., *Cancer Discovery*.
- **2013**: Second major publication — Gao et al., *Science Signaling*.
- **2023**: Third major publication — de Bruijn et al., *Cancer Research*.

## How to Cite cBioPortal

When using cBioPortal in publications, cite these three papers:

1. Cerami et al. "The cBio Cancer Genomics Portal: An Open Platform for Exploring Multidimensional Cancer Genomics Data." *Cancer Discovery* 2, 401–404 (2012). doi:10.1158/2159-8290.CD-12-0095
2. Gao et al. "Integrative Analysis of Complex Cancer Genomics and Clinical Profiles Using the cBioPortal." *Science Signaling* 6, pl1 (2013). doi:10.1126/scisignal.2004088
3. de Bruijn et al. "Analysis and Visualization of Longitudinal Genomic and Clinical Data from the AACR Project GENIE Biopharma Collaborative in cBioPortal." *Cancer Research* 83, 3861–3867 (2023). doi:10.1158/0008-5472.CAN-23-0816

Also cite the specific study publication(s) whose data you used.

## What Data Types Does cBioPortal Contain?

- **Mutations** (non-synonymous somatic mutations)
- **DNA copy-number alterations** (CNA) from GISTIC or other methods
- **mRNA expression** data (RNA-seq, microarray)
- **Protein/phosphoprotein** levels (RPPA, mass spectrometry)
- **DNA methylation** data
- **Structural variants** (gene fusions, rearrangements)
- **Clinical data** (demographics, diagnosis, treatment, outcomes)

Note: Synonymous mutations are not included in cBioPortal.

## What Cancer Types Are in the Database?

Answer from the studies actually loaded, not from the OncoTree ontology (OncoTree lists hundreds of codes, most with no study here). One query:

```sql
SELECT tc.type_of_cancer_id, tc.name, tc.tissue, count() AS studies
FROM cancer_study cs
JOIN type_of_cancer tc ON cs.type_of_cancer_id = tc.type_of_cancer_id
GROUP BY tc.type_of_cancer_id, tc.name, tc.tissue
ORDER BY studies DESC;
```

Report how many distinct cancer types there are across how many studies, grouped by `tissue`. `mixed` ("Mixed Cancer Types") marks multi-cancer studies such as MSK-IMPACT and MSK-CHORD; their per-sample cancer types are in `clinical_data_derived` (`CANCER_TYPE`).

## Data Types Usually Not Stored Directly

cBioPortal generally does not store:

- raw CT, MRI, pathology-slide, or histology image files
- raw sequencing files such as BAM, CRAM, or FASTQ
- polygenic risk scores as a standard data type
- full external clinical-trial databases

It depends on where the data is going. Which of these fits your case?

1. **Submit to the public cBioPortal.** Follow the cBioPortal data curation and DataHub process, and format your files to the DataHub specifications.
2. **Load into a private or local cBioPortal instance.** Build a study package and import it with the portal's importer.
3. **Visualize your own data without a full study.** Standalone tools such as Mutation Mapper and OncoPrint may be enough.

For options 1 and 2, clinical data goes into a study folder with these files.

**Required files**
- **`meta_study.txt`** has the study ID, name, cancer type and description.
- **`meta_clinical_patient.txt`** and **`data_clinical_patient.txt`** hold patient-level data, such as age, sex, overall survival and vital status.
- **`meta_clinical_sample.txt`** and **`data_clinical_sample.txt`** hold sample-level data, such as cancer type, OncoTree code and sample type.
- **A case list** of the sample IDs in the study. Importers usually generate this for you.

**Format of the clinical data files**
- The files are tab-delimited.
- The first 4 lines are `#`-prefixed headers, in this order:
  - display name
  - description
  - datatype (`STRING`, `NUMBER` or `BOOLEAN`)
  - priority
- The fifth line holds the column names, in uppercase.
- The patient file needs `PATIENT_ID`. The sample file needs `PATIENT_ID` and `SAMPLE_ID`.
- Use the standard attribute names so the portal recognizes them:
  - Survival: `OS_STATUS` (`0:LIVING` or `1:DECEASED`) and `OS_MONTHS`. The same pattern applies to `DFS_*` and `PFS_*`.
  - Cancer type: `CANCER_TYPE`, `CANCER_TYPE_DETAILED` and `ONCOTREE_CODE`.
  - Age and sex: `AGE` and `SEX`.
- Missing values can be left empty or written as `NA`.

**Practical tips**
- Put patient-level attributes in the patient file and sample-level attributes in the sample file. This keeps the study view and the survival analyses correct.
- Run the portal's validator (`validateData.py`) before importing. It catches most formatting problems.
- Keep sample and patient IDs consistent across every file in the study, including any mutation or copy-number files.
- Be careful with de-identification. Dates and exact ages are often shifted, floored or capped. The portal displays whatever you load, so apply your institution's privacy rules first.
- For longitudinal data such as treatments, specimens and lab values, use the timeline file format. It is separate from the clinical files.
