**Potential methodological concerns**

1. **NLP-derived variables.** Several clinical fields are extracted from free text, including treatment, radiology progression, and some biomarker status. The database guide flags these as NLP-derived with possible extraction errors. Accuracy may differ by cancer type, institution period, or note style, so any association involving these variables could carry differential misclassification.

2. **Missing treatment equals no treatment.** The guide notes that the data cannot distinguish "not collected" from "not received." Treatment-based analyses, such as immunotherapy versus none, are therefore vulnerable to misclassifying untreated or unrecorded patients.

3. **Immunotherapy response is proxied.** The database has no RECIST or best-response field. Outcomes rely on overall survival or NLP-derived progression events. Those proxies are coarser than trial-grade response data and are sensitive to how "line of therapy" and treatment start are defined.

4. **Confounding in observational survival and treatment comparisons.** This is a retrospective, single-institution cohort. Treatment choice tracks performance status, stage, and molecular features. Multivariable adjustment can only address measured confounders, and the paper's conclusions about biomarkers and outcomes should be read with that limit in mind.

5. **Targeted-panel heterogeneity.** The cohort spans four MSK-IMPACT versions (341, 410, 468, and 505 genes). Gene coverage differs by sample. Genes added in later panels have shorter observation windows, so frequency comparisons across time need gene-specific denominators. TMB calculated on different panel sizes is noisier on smaller panels and needs normalization.

6. **Unit of analysis.** The guide reports 24,950 patients and 25,040 samples, with 90 multi-sample patients, all from different cancer types. Pooling samples as independent observations can inflate effective sample size. Any analysis that counts samples rather than patients should say which unit it uses.

7. **Pooled co-occurrence and mutual exclusivity.** Pan-cancer co-mutation tests can show Simpson's-paradox effects, because some gene pairs are enriched in particular cancer types. Results should be stratified by cancer type, with multiple-testing correction across many gene pairs.

8. **Selection bias.** Patients were sequenced at a referral center, often at later stages or after clinical need. The cohort is not a population sample, and its ancestry and demographic composition may limit generalizability.

9. **Time origin and guarantee-time bias.** Survival time may start at diagnosis, at sequencing, or at treatment start. Those choices change the comparison, and patients must survive long enough to receive a treatment, which can bias treatment-versus-outcome comparisons.

10. **Germline and somatic classification.** Germline filtering depends on matched normals and on pipeline versions that changed over the study period. Residual germline variants can distort variant allele fraction and driver-gene analyses.

11. **Reproducibility.** The data are licensed CC BY-NC-ND and require a request for commercial use. Independent reanalysis is therefore constrained, which matters given the NLP-derived variables.

**What would strengthen the study:** a manual-review validation subset with reported NLP accuracy per variable, sensitivity analyses that treat unknown treatment as a separate category, and per-cancer-type reporting of co-occurrence and survival results.
