## What I can ground in the cBioPortal data

I can confirm the cohort structure the paper was built on, and a few features of it are worth flagging as genuine methodological considerations:

1. **The cohort is restricted to five cancer types.** The `msk_chord_2024` dataset is exactly five: NSCLC (7,809), colorectal (5,543), breast (5,368), prostate (3,211), and pancreatic (3,109) — totaling ~24,950 patients / 25,040 samples. There is no melanoma, no sarcoma, no hematologic malignancy, no other solid type. A "cross-cancer" study that omits entire lineages (e.g., no melanoma in a pan-tumor mutation-frequency study) is a real scope limitation, not a full pan-cancer survey.

2. **Almost one tumor sample per patient.** Only 90 of 24,950 patients have more than one sample, and just 26 have both a primary and a metastasis. So the study is fundamentally a cross-sectional snapshot; any claim about intra-patient evolution, clonal selection, or treatment-emergent resistance from this cohort would be unsupported — there is no meaningful paired primary-vs-metastasis cohort in the data.

3. **Variable gene coverage across samples due to panel evolution.** The dataset spans four MSK-IMPACT panel versions (341/410/468/505 genes). Any cross-cancer or cross-time comparison of mutation frequencies is confounded unless gene-specific denominators are used — a sample sequenced on the 341-gene panel cannot be scored against a gene absent from it. This is a genuine comparability flaw inherent to a longitudinal single-center cohort.

4. **NLP-derived annotations.** Several clinical/treatment fields are extracted by NLP and carry an `INFERRED_TX_PROB` confidence flag. NLP extraction has known error rates; treatment/timing fields are only as reliable as the inference. This is a data-quality caveat, not a fabrication claim on my part — it's documented in the study's own metadata.
