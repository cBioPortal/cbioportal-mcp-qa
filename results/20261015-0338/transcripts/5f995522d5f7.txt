I can offer a critique from general knowledge of the MSK-CHORD study design and of real-world genomic cohort studies. I haven't re-read the paper or checked its supplementary methods in this session, so verify any specific number or method detail against the published text.

**Main methodological concerns**

1. **Selection bias.** MSK-CHORD includes patients with metastatic solid tumors sequenced with MSK-IMPACT at a single academic center. Whether a patient was sequenced depends on physician ordering, referral patterns, and how long the patient survived. Patients who were sequenced may therefore differ systematically from the general metastatic population.

2. **Time-zero and immortal-time bias.** Survival is measured from a start point that varies by patient, such as metastatic diagnosis, first treatment, or sequencing date. If the start point is sequencing, patients must have survived long enough to be sequenced, which biases survival estimates upward. Landmark analyses or a consistent origin would reduce this problem.

3. **EHR-derived outcomes.** Overall survival depends on death ascertainment from health records and external registries, which can be incomplete or delayed. Loss to follow-up can be informative, and censoring may not be random.

4. **Confounding by treatment and era.** The cohort spans many years of changing standard of care, including the immunotherapy era. Genotype-outcome associations can reflect treatment exposure, line of therapy, or calendar time rather than biology. Unless these are adjusted for, the associations are hard to interpret. Treatment capture from real-world records is also often incomplete or abstracted inconsistently.

5. **Pan-cancer pooling.** Pooling many tumor types can create Simpson's-paradox-type effects, where a gene's association differs by cancer type but the pooled estimate is dominated by the largest cancer types. Stratified or cancer-type-adjusted models are needed to separate these.

6. **Multiple testing.** Screening many gene-by-cancer-type combinations raises the rate of false positives. Small subgroups are especially prone to unstable estimates, so the correction approach and minimum group size matter.

7. **Panel and TMB issues.** MSK-IMPACT panel versions changed over time, so gene coverage differs between samples. TMB estimates depend on the effective callable region, which varies with panel version. Tumor-only germline filtering also introduces classification errors, and some variants may be misclassified as somatic.

8. **Clinical variable quality.** Stage, performance status, histology, and smoking history often come from NLP or manual abstraction, which carries missingness and measurement error. How missing values were handled (complete-case analysis, imputation, or an "unknown" category) affects the results.

9. **Generalizability.** The patient population is largely from one institution and region, with its own demographic and socioeconomic profile. Findings may not transfer to other health systems.

10. **Model assumptions.** Whether proportional hazards assumptions were tested, and how time-varying covariates were handled, affect the hazard ratios. These details should be checked in the supplement.

**What would strengthen the work**

- Pre-specified time origins with landmark sensitivity analyses
- Multivariable models adjusting for treatment, line, era, age, sex, stage, and cancer type
- Reporting of the full cohort versus the sequenced subset, with comparisons between them
- Sensitivity analyses across MSK-IMPACT panel versions
- External validation in an independent cohort
